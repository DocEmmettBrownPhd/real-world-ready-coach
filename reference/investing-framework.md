## The Core Principle

Investing is not about getting rich fast. It is about building wealth slowly — by starting early and staying consistent.

The single most powerful force in investing is **compound interest**: earning returns on both your original money and the returns it has already generated. The earlier you start, the longer this force works for you.

**The number that matters most is not how much you invest. It is how early you start.**

---

## The Right Order of Operations

Before investing, three things must be in place:

1. **Income established** — know your real take-home pay (Branch 2)
2. **Bank account open** — checking and savings accounts active (Branch 3)
3. **Emergency fund started** — minimum $500 saved before investing begins (Branch 3)

Only after those three are in place does investing make sense. Investing before an emergency fund means the first unexpected expense forces you to sell at a bad time.

## How Much to Invest

### The Standard Target
Financial experts — including CFPB, NEFE, and major wealth management firms — consistently recommend **10–15% of take-home income** toward long-term savings and investing combined.

### The Entry Point for Beginners
For young adults just starting out, **5% of take-home pay** is the realistic starting point:
- It builds the habit without creating financial strain
- It is small enough to maintain every paycheck
- It grows substantially over time due to compound interest
- It can be increased as income grows

### The Goal Progression
| Stage | Investing % | Notes |
|-------|------------|-------|
| Starting out | 5% of take-home | After emergency fund started |
| Stable footing | 10% of take-home | When budget is working consistently |
| Full target | 15% of take-home | Combined savings + investing goal |

### Real Numbers Example
Take-home pay: $1,200/month

| % | Monthly investment | Annual | 10 years (7% avg return) | 30 years (7% avg return) |
|---|-------------------|--------|--------------------------|--------------------------|
| 5% | $60 | $720 | ~$9,900 | ~$68,000 |
| 10% | $120 | $1,440 | ~$19,800 | ~$136,000 |
| 15% | $180 | $2,160 | ~$29,700 | ~$204,000 |

*These are approximate projections using a 7% average annual return, consistent with historical S&P 500 long-term averages.*

## What to Invest In: Index Funds

### What an Index Fund Is
An index fund is a type of investment that automatically buys a small piece of hundreds or thousands of companies at once. Instead of betting on one company, you own a tiny slice of the entire market.

The most common index funds track the **S&P 500** — the 500 largest companies in the United States.

### Why Index Funds for Beginners
- **Low cost** — expense ratios as low as 0.03%–0.05% per year
- **Diversified** — spreading money across hundreds of companies reduces risk
- **Proven performance** — the S&P 500 has averaged approximately 7–10% annual returns over the long term (inflation-adjusted: ~7%)
- **No expertise required** — no need to research individual companies
- **Backed by research** — Vanguard founder John Bogle's research showed that most professional fund managers underperform simple index funds over time

### Recommended Beginner Index Funds on Robinhood
| Fund | What It Tracks | Expense Ratio |
|------|---------------|---------------|
| **VOO** (Vanguard S&P 500 ETF) | S&P 500 — 500 largest U.S. companies | 0.03% |

| **SPY** (SPDR S&P 500 ETF) | S&P 500 | 0.09% |

**For most beginners: VOO or VTI. Pick one. Do not switch back and forth.**

## How to Set Up a Robinhood Account

### Why Robinhood for Beginners
- Free trades (no commissions)
- Fractional shares — invest as little as $1
- Mobile-first — built for phone users
- No account minimums
- Available on iOS and Android

### Step-by-Step Setup
1. Download the Robinhood app (iOS or Android)
2. Tap **Sign Up**
3. Enter your name, email, and create a password
4. Enter your date of birth, address, and last 4 digits of Social Security Number (required by law — this is standard for all brokerage accounts)
5. Answer the employment and financial experience questions honestly
6. Link your bank account (checking account from Branch 3)
7. Wait 1–3 business days for identity verification
8. Fund your account — transfer your first investment amount from your bank
9. Search for **VOO** or **VTI**
10. Tap **Buy** — choose a dollar amount (not shares) — enter your 5% amount
11. Set a recurring investment if available — same amount, every payday

### What Robinhood Will Ask
- Social Security Number — required by law (SEC/FINRA regulations) for all brokerage accounts
- Employment status — answer honestly
- Investment experience — beginner is fine, answer accurately
- Annual income range — approximate is okay

## The Mindset Rules

### Rule 1: Time in the market beats timing the market
Do not wait for the "right time" to invest. The best time was yesterday. The second-best time is today. Trying to predict when prices will be low is a losing strategy even for professionals.

### Rule 2: Never sell when the market drops
Markets go down. Sometimes significantly. This is normal. A 20% drop does not mean your money is gone — it means shares are temporarily cheaper. Selling during a drop locks in the loss permanently. Staying invested means the recovery works for you.

### Rule 3: Invest every paycheck — not lump sums
This is called **dollar-cost averaging**. By investing the same amount every payday, you automatically buy more shares when prices are low and fewer when prices are high. Over time this smooths out the volatility.

### Rule 4: Do not check it every day
Looking at your investment balance daily leads to emotional decisions. Check it monthly at most. The goal is decades from now, not next week.

### Rule 5: No individual stock picking yet
Picking individual companies — even famous ones — requires research, risk tolerance, and experience. Index funds give the same market exposure with far less risk. Individual stocks come later, if at all.

## The Compound Interest Argument

This is the most powerful case for starting early. Use real numbers.

**Scenario: $60/month ($720/year) invested at 7% average annual return**

| Start Age | Stop Contributing | End Age | Total Invested | Estimated Value at 65 |
|-----------|------------------|---------|----------------|----------------------|
| 22 | Never | 65 | $27,360 | ~$185,000 |
| 22 | Age 32 (10 years only) | 65 | $7,200 | ~$115,000 |
| 32 | Never | 65 | $23,760 | ~$85,000 |
| 40 | Never | 65 | $18,000 | ~$45,000 |

The person who starts at 22 and stops at 32 ends up with more than the person who starts at 32 and never stops — because of the extra 10 years of compounding.

**That is the argument. Starting early — even small — beats starting late with more.**

## Key Vocabulary (Plain English)

| Term | Plain English |
|------|--------------|
| Index fund | A basket that holds hundreds of stocks at once |
| ETF | A fund you buy like a stock — trades on the stock market |
| S&P 500 | The 500 biggest U.S. companies tracked as one index |
| Expense ratio | The annual fee the fund charges (0.03% = $0.30 per $1,000) |
| Fractional share | Owning a piece of one share — lets you invest $1 instead of $400 |
| Dollar-cost averaging | Investing the same amount on the same schedule regardless of price |
| Compound interest | Earning returns on your returns — money growing on itself |
| Brokerage account | The account that holds your investments (Robinhood is one) |
| Dividend | When a company pays you a small cash amount for holding its stock |
| Volatility | How much prices go up and down — normal, not a reason to sell |
