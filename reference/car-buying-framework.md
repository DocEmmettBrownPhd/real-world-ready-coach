# Car Buying Framework

Buying a car is one of the biggest financial decisions a young adult will make. The coach goes deep here — not surface level.

**Primary sources:** CFPB auto loan guidance; Edmunds True Cost to Own methodology; NADA guides; FTC *Buying a Used Car*; Insurance Institute for Highway Safety (IIHS) safety ratings.

---

## Core Principle

Most people shop by monthly payment. That is exactly how dealers get people into loans they cannot afford.

The CFPB specifically warns that a lower monthly payment achieved by extending the loan term (e.g., from 48 to 72 months) can add thousands of dollars in total interest while also increasing the risk of being upside-down on the loan (owing more than the car is worth).

The coach protects the user by focusing on total cost, full ownership cost, and whether the user is financially ready to buy at all.

---

## Step 1: Establish Readiness

Before talking about any specific car, ask:
1. What is your monthly take-home pay?
2. How much do you have saved for a down payment?
3. Do you know your credit score? (Check free at AnnualCreditReport.com)
4. Do you have someone who could cosign if your credit is limited?
5. Have you factored in insurance, gas, and maintenance on top of the payment?
6. Do you need the car for work? What happens if it breaks down?

If the user cannot answer questions 1–3, the conversation starts there — not at the car.

---

## Step 2: Affordability Rule

General coaching guideline:
- Car payment should not exceed 15% of monthly take-home pay
- Total transportation costs (payment + insurance + gas + maintenance) should not exceed 20–25% of take-home pay

Example:
> Take-home pay is $2,000/month. The car payment should be $300 or less. Then add insurance ($100–$200 for a young driver), gas ($80–$150), and a $75/month maintenance reserve. Total transportation cost: $555–$725/month — already 28–36% of take-home. That is the reality before choosing a car that is too expensive.

**Edmunds TCO note:** Edmunds True Cost to Own includes depreciation, taxes, financing, insurance, fuel, maintenance, and repairs over 5 years. For a $20,000 car, the 5-year true cost often exceeds $35,000. Coach the user to think in total cost, not sticker price.

---

## Step 3: New vs. Used

**New car:**
- Higher purchase price
- Full manufacturer warranty
- Latest safety features (IIHS top safety picks change annually — newer cars often have better ratings)
- Depreciates fastest in the first two years — loses 15–25% of value in year one
- Best if buying through a manufacturer incentive program with 0% financing

**Used car:**
- Lower purchase price
- Reduced or no remaining warranty (check what remains on factory warranty)
- Unknown history unless a vehicle history report (Carfax or AutoCheck) is obtained
- Can represent excellent value if inspected by a mechanic before purchase

**Coaching default for first-time buyers:** A 2–4 year old used car from a reputable dealer or private seller, with a vehicle history report and a pre-purchase inspection, is typically the strongest value for a young adult on a limited budget.

**FTC guidance:** Every used car sold by a dealer must display a Buyers Guide. Read it. "As Is" means no warranty — any problem after the sale is the buyer's responsibility.

---

## Step 4: Understanding the Loan

Teach these terms before any paperwork:

- **APR (Annual Percentage Rate):** The true cost of borrowing, including interest and fees. A lower APR means less money paid over the life of the loan.
- **Loan term:** How many months to pay it off. Longer terms mean lower monthly payments but significantly more total interest.
- **Total cost calculation:** Monthly payment × number of months = what the car actually costs. Always calculate this.
- **Down payment:** Reduces the financed amount, reduces total interest paid, and reduces the risk of being upside-down.
- **GAP insurance:** Covers the difference between the loan balance and the car's value if it is totaled or stolen. Especially important if the down payment is small or the loan term is long. Often cheaper through an insurance company than through the dealer.

### Loan Term Reality
*(Based on CFPB auto loan research)*

| Loan Term | Monthly Payment on $15,000 at 8% APR | Total Interest Paid |
|-----------|--------------------------------------|---------------------|
| 36 months | $470 | $1,920 |
| 48 months | $366 | $2,570 |
| 60 months | $304 | $3,240 |
| 72 months | $263 | $3,940 |

The longer term feels cheaper monthly. It costs significantly more overall.

### Loan Red Flags
- Loan term longer than 60 months on a used car
- APR above 10% without a plan to refinance once credit improves
- Add-ons (paint protection, tire warranty, fabric protection) added without clear explanation
- Monthly payment presented without total cost or APR
- Terms verbally agreed on that differ from the written contract
- Pressure to sign the same day without time to review

## Step 5: Cosigner Coaching (Car)

This conversation is kept entirely separate from the apartment cosigner conversation. Do not carry assumptions between them.

Ask in sequence:
1. Do you have someone who trusts you enough to cosign?
2. Does that person understand they are equally and fully responsible for this loan — not a backup, a co-borrower?
3. What happens to that relationship if you miss payments? Have you thought through that honestly?
4. Would a cosigner help you qualify for a significantly lower interest rate? (It often does — this is worth quantifying.)
5. Would building credit for 6–12 months first be a better path than asking someone to cosign now?

**Legal note:** A cosigner on a car loan is equally liable for the full balance. Late payments appear on both the primary borrower's and cosigner's credit reports. Make sure both parties understand this fully.

---

## Step 6: Pre-Purchase Inspection

For used cars — non-negotiable recommendation.

- An independent mechanic inspection (not the dealer's mechanic) typically costs $100–$150
- Can identify issues that cost thousands — transmission problems, rust, frame damage, worn brakes
- If the seller refuses to allow an independent inspection, treat that as a significant red flag
- Pull a vehicle history report (Carfax or AutoCheck) before the inspection — check for accidents, title issues, odometer discrepancies, number of previous owners

**FTC note:** A clean Carfax report does not guarantee a clean car — it only reports incidents that were reported. The mechanic inspection is still essential.

---

## Step 7: Ongoing Maintenance

Once the car is purchased, the coaching continues:

- Oil change schedule: every 3,000–5,000 miles for conventional oil, 7,500–10,000 for full synthetic (check the owner's manual — it is always the authoritative source)
- Tire pressure: check monthly — underinflated tires reduce fuel efficiency and increase wear
- Tire rotation: every 5,000–7,500 miles
- Brake inspection: annually or at the first sign of squealing or vibration
- Check engine light: never ignore it — some triggers are minor, but some indicate serious damage that worsens with delay
- Maintenance fund: set aside $50–$100/month from the start. Unexpected repairs are not unexpected over time — they are certainties.
