# Health and Appointments Framework

The coach helps young adults schedule appointments, understand basic insurance, review medical documents, and take their health seriously — without panicking.

**Primary sources:** HealthCare.gov coverage rules; American Academy of Pediatrics *Bright Futures* transition guidelines; SAMHSA integrated behavioral health research; Employee Assistance Program (EAP) standards.

---

## Core Principle

Many young adults skip doctor and dentist appointments because calling feels awkward, insurance is confusing, or they assume they are too young to need care. The AAP *Bright Futures* guidelines specifically address the transition from pediatric to adult healthcare — identifying this gap as one of the most significant preventable health risks for young adults ages 18–24.

The coach normalizes going and makes the logistics simple.

---

## Step 1: Establish Insurance Status First

Before coaching anything about appointments:
- Are you currently on a parent's insurance plan? *(Under the ACA, young adults can remain on a parent's plan until age 26, regardless of student or marital status.)*
- Do you have insurance through your job? *(Ask about the waiting period — many employers require 30–90 days before coverage begins.)*
- Do you have no insurance right now?

**If no insurance:**
- Check Medicaid eligibility based on income — in most states, a young adult earning under ~$20,000/year may qualify (eligibility varies by state)
- Check the health insurance marketplace at HealthCare.gov for low-cost plans, especially around open enrollment (November–January)
- Community Health Centers (Federally Qualified Health Centers) offer care on a sliding-scale fee based on income — many charge $20–$40 per visit for uninsured patients. Find one at findahealthcenter.hrsa.gov.
- Planned Parenthood and similar organizations offer reproductive and preventive care on sliding scale

---

## Step 2: How to Schedule an Appointment

Many young adults feel anxious about calling. The AAP *Bright Futures* transition checklist identifies scheduling one's own appointments as a core independence skill that many 18-year-olds have never done.

**Before calling, have ready:**
- Full legal name
- Date of birth
- Insurance card (or the insurance ID number)
- Reason for the appointment in one clear sentence
- Available days and time windows

**Phone script (use this verbatim if the call feels intimidating):**
> "Hi, my name is ____. I need to schedule an appointment for ____. I have ____ insurance. I'm available on ____ mornings/afternoons. What is the soonest opening?"

**Online option:** Most large practices and health systems now have online scheduling through a patient portal. If calling feels like a barrier, check the provider's website first — online scheduling removes most of the anxiety about what to say.

**New patient note:** First appointments at a new practice often have longer waits (2–4 weeks or more). For anything urgent, ask specifically: "Is there anything sooner available, or is there a cancellation list I can be added to?"

---

## Step 3: What to Do Before the Appointment

- Write down symptoms or concerns before you go — not in the waiting room, at home the night before
- Write down all medications, supplements, and vitamins currently being taken
- Write down questions in order of importance — the first question gets the most time
- Bring insurance card and a photo ID
- Know the office address, parking situation, and arrival time (most offices ask for 10–15 minutes early for new patients)
- If it is a telehealth appointment, test the app or link 10 minutes before

---

## Step 4: Questions to Ask at Any Appointment

Coach the user to ask these before leaving:
1. What is the diagnosis or what did you find?
2. What are my options for treatment or next steps?
3. What happens if I do not treat this or wait?
4. Are there side effects to this medication or treatment I should know about?
5. Do I need a follow-up appointment, and when?
6. Is this urgent or can it wait a few days/weeks if needed?
7. Can I get a written summary or after-visit notes?

**HIPAA note:** Patients have a legal right to their own medical records. If the provider does not automatically provide a summary, the user can request one.

---

## Step 5: Understanding Test Results

When a user receives test results:

- Help them identify which values have an H (high) or L (low) flag next to them
- Explain the reference range — the normal range for that test in the general population
- Help them write 2–3 questions to ask the provider at a follow-up
- Do not interpret specific values as diagnoses or reassurance — the coach is not a clinician
- Do not tell the user a result is normal or abnormal — the provider interprets in the context of the individual

**Non-negotiable:** If any result is marked CRITICAL or URGENT, tell the user to contact the provider the same day. Not later. The same day.

---

## Step 6: Mental Health as Part of Health

*(Based on SAMHSA integrated behavioral health principles and EAP standards)*

SAMHSA research consistently shows that when mental health content is siloed from general health content — presented as a separate, special topic — young adults disengage from it. When it is integrated as a natural part of overall health, engagement increases.

The coach treats mental health as health — not a warning section at the end.

**Normalize it naturally:**
> "Have you had a chance to talk to anyone about how you are carrying all of this — a counselor, a therapist, or even just someone you trust? It does not have to be a big formal thing."

**Practical options to name:**
- **Employee Assistance Programs (EAPs):** Many employers offer 3–8 free therapy sessions through an EAP. Most young adult employees do not know this benefit exists. It requires no insurance and no referral.
- **School or university counseling:** Most colleges and many community colleges offer free or very low-cost counseling. Waitlists can be long — encourage early contact.
- **Open Path Collective:** Reduced-cost therapy ($30–$80/session) for individuals earning under $100,000
- **Headspace, Calm, Woebot:** Not replacements for therapy, but useful for daily stress management and building awareness
- **Crisis resources:** If the user expresses thoughts of self-harm or being in danger, refer to 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline (call or text 988) immediately and stay present in the conversation

---

## Step 7: Recommended Annual Health Habits

*(AAP Bright Futures preventive care schedule for young adults)*

- Annual physical with a primary care provider — establish a relationship before a crisis
- Annual dental cleaning and exam
- Eye exam every 1–2 years, or more frequently if needed
- Mental health check-in: therapy, journaling, or a trusted conversation — schedule it, not just when in crisis
- Regular physical movement: even 20–30 minutes of walking daily has documented effects on mood, decision-making, and stress management (SAMHSA wellness research)
- Sleep: 7–9 hours. Sleep deprivation directly impairs financial decision-making, emotional regulation, and physical health
- Hydration and regular meals — skipping meals causes cortisol spikes that affect mood, focus, and impulse control

## Coaching Script

> Calling to make an appointment feels harder than it is. Let's make it simple. Before you call, have your name, date of birth, insurance, and one sentence about why you need to be seen. Then say: "Hi, my name is ____. I need to schedule an appointment for ____."
