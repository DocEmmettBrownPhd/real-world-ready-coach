# Grocery Framework

The goal is not to give the user a perfect meal plan. The goal is to build one they will actually follow.

**Primary sources:** USDA Thrifty Food Plan; USDA MyPlate guidelines; Cooperative Extension Service household budgeting curricula (land-grant universities).

---

## Core Principle

Eating out every day is one of the fastest ways to lose money without noticing it. The USDA Thrifty Food Plan — the lowest-cost nutritionally adequate diet — assumes most meals are prepared at home. A young adult eating out for most meals can spend 3–5x the cost of cooking the same meals at home.

The coach does not try to overhaul the user's eating habits all at once. The goal is to replace the most expensive eating-out habit first, with the simplest possible alternative.

---

## Step 1: Assess the Situation Before Recommending Anything

1. What cooking equipment do you have — stove, microwave, air fryer, toaster, fridge? (The answer changes everything about what is realistic to recommend.)
2. What are three foods you actually like and would eat regularly?
3. How many days a week are you currently eating out?
4. What is your rough weekly food budget?
5. Do you have roommates who share food costs or cooking?
6. Do you have transportation to a grocery store, or are you limited to what is nearby?

**Cooperative Extension note:** Transportation access to full grocery stores is a real barrier for many young adults, especially in urban areas without cars. If the user can only access a corner store or dollar store, the framework must adapt to what is actually available.

---

## Step 2: The Beginner Grocery List

Built from the USDA Thrifty Food Plan's lowest-cost food groups, selected for ease of preparation and flexibility.

**Protein (cheapest per gram):**
- Eggs (most versatile, lowest cost per serving)
- Canned tuna or chicken (no cooking required, shelf-stable)
- Rotisserie chicken (pre-cooked, multiple meals possible)
- Ground beef or ground turkey
- Canned or dried beans and lentils (among the cheapest protein sources per serving)

**Carbohydrates:**
- White or brown rice (cheap, filling, stores well)
- Pasta
- Bread
- Tortillas (versatile — wraps, quesadillas, breakfast burritos)
- Oats (one of the cheapest breakfasts available)

**Vegetables:**
- Frozen vegetables — same nutritional value as fresh, no spoilage, much cheaper per serving
- Bagged salad for days when cooking is not happening
- Whatever is on sale fresh — rotate based on price

**Staples and condiments:**
- Olive oil or cooking spray
- Salt, pepper, garlic powder, cumin — four seasonings that cover most meals
- Jarred pasta sauce (cheap, versatile)
- Peanut butter (high protein, shelf-stable, no cooking)
- Canned soup or broth (quick meal or base for cooking)
- Hot sauce (makes simple food interesting)

---

## Step 3: Simple Meals That Require Minimal Skill

All of these can be made with beginner-level cooking ability and basic equipment:

- Scrambled eggs and toast (5 minutes, under $1)
- Rice and canned beans with hot sauce (10 minutes, under $1.50)
- Pasta with jarred sauce and ground beef (20 minutes, under $3)
- Rotisserie chicken with frozen vegetables microwaved (5 minutes, under $4)
- Peanut butter and banana on bread (2 minutes, under $0.75)
- Oatmeal with peanut butter stirred in (5 minutes, under $0.50)
- Quesadilla with chicken, cheese, and salsa (10 minutes, under $2)
- Tuna with crackers and hot sauce (no cooking, under $2)
- Canned soup with extra frozen vegetables and rice added (15 minutes, under $3)

---

## Step 4: Cost Comparison Coaching

Make the math visible. USDA Thrifty Food Plan research shows that most young adults significantly underestimate how much eating out costs until they see the comparison written out.

Example:
> A $12 lunch five days a week = $60/week = $240/month = $2,880/year.
> A weekly grocery run for $60–$80 can cover most meals for the week.
> That is $160–$180 saved every single month.

**Coaching approach:** Do not tell the user to stop eating out entirely. That almost never works. Instead:
> Replace just two eating-out meals this week with something from the list above. That alone could save $20–$25 in a week.

Start there. Build from there.

---

## Step 5: Reducing Food Waste

Food waste is one of the biggest budget leaks for beginner cooks. Cooperative Extension research shows that beginner households waste 20–30% of purchased food.

Strategies:
- Buy frozen vegetables instead of fresh when possible — no spoilage
- Rotisserie chicken becomes dinner on night one, wraps or rice bowls on night two, soup base on night three
- Cook extra portions intentionally — leftovers are not leftovers, they are tomorrow's lunch
- Keep "backup meals" for days when energy is low: oatmeal, canned soup, eggs — things that require almost no effort
- Check the fridge before shopping — buy what is missing, not a new set of everything
- Bagged salad has a short shelf life — buy it only when it will be eaten within 2–3 days

---

## Unit Price Coaching

The price on the shelf is not always the best way to compare items. Teach the user to check the unit price (price per ounce, per serving, or per count) shown on most store shelf labels.

Example:
> A 16-oz bag of pasta for $1.29 costs $0.08/oz.
> A 32-oz bag of pasta for $2.19 costs $0.068/oz.
> The larger bag costs less per ounce — if the user will actually use it all.

Buying bulk only saves money if the food does not go to waste before it is used.

---

## Coaching Script

> I do not want to give you a meal plan you will quit in three days. I want to find the two or three meals you like, that you can actually make with what you have, that replace the most expensive eating-out habit. What do you actually like to eat, and what equipment do you have to work with?
