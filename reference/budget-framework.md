# Budget Framework

The goal is not to make the user feel restricted. The goal is to help them control their money before it disappears.

**Primary source:** CFPB *Your Money, Your Goals* toolkit; 50/30/20 framework (Warren & Tyagi, *All Your Worth*, 2005); NEFE research on young adult financial behavior.

---

## Step 1: Establish Income First

Before building any budget, confirm:
- Source of income: parents, job, or both
- Exact take-home amount after taxes
- Pay frequency: weekly, biweekly, monthly
- Whether income is reliable or varies week to week

Never build a budget around an estimated or hoped-for number. CFPB research shows young adults consistently overestimate take-home pay when they forget taxes, Social Security, and Medicare withholdings.

**Coaching note:** If the user gives a vague number, use Rule 6 (Vague Number Protocol) — push once for the exact figure, then accept an estimate and label it clearly.

---

## Step 2: List Fixed Obligations

These come out before anything else:
- Rent or housing contribution
- Car payment
- Insurance (car, renter's, health if applicable)
- Phone bill
- Subscriptions (essential ones only — audit the rest)
- Debt minimum payments

Fixed obligations are non-negotiable. They leave first. Whatever is left is the real budget.

---

## Step 3: List Variable Necessities

- Food and groceries
- Gas or public transportation
- Utilities if not included in rent
- Basic personal care

These vary month to month. Coach the user to estimate from last month's actual spending, not from what they hope to spend.

---

## Step 4: Protect Emergency Money

Goal: 3 months of expenses. Starting point: whatever they can.

Even $20 per paycheck matters. The Federal Reserve reports that nearly 40% of Americans cannot cover a $400 emergency without borrowing — young adults leaving home are especially exposed.

Coach the user to treat savings as a fixed obligation, not as whatever is left over. If it is not automatic, it usually does not happen.

**Sequence:** Open savings account first → set up automatic transfer on payday → treat it as untouchable.

---

## Step 5: What Is Left Is Spending Money

Anything left after fixed obligations, variable necessities, and savings is available for discretionary spending.

If nothing is left — that is the conversation to have. Do not skip it. The 50/30/20 framework provides useful proportions (50% needs, 30% wants, 20% savings/debt), but it only works once income is verified and fixed costs are confirmed. For a young adult with high fixed costs relative to income, the percentages will not match. That is okay. Adjust to the real numbers.

---

## Step 6: Beginner Budget Priority Order

Before spending on wants, protect:
1. Housing
2. Food
3. Transportation to work or school
4. Phone
5. Required bills and debt minimums
6. Emergency savings (even a small amount)

This order comes from CFPB emergency financial coaching protocols, which sequence obligations by survival priority rather than financial optimality.

---

## Coaching Warnings — Watch For These

The patterns below consistently appear in NEFE research on young adult financial failure in the first two years of independence:

- Spending before bills are handled
- Shopping based on emotion or boredom
- Overdraft fees eating the account monthly
- Subscriptions the user forgot they have (audit these — stream services, apps, gym memberships)
- Car payment consuming more than 15% of take-home pay
- Rent consuming more than 30–35% of take-home pay
- No emergency cushion at all — one car repair away from crisis
- Credit card balance growing every month without a payoff plan
- Buy now, pay later debt piling up outside the budget
- Payday loans or high-interest short-term loans (APR often 300–400%)
- Ignoring bills because they feel overwhelming

---

## Credit Card in the Budget

A credit card is not extra money. It is a delayed bill.

Coach the user to:
- Only charge what they already have the cash to pay
- Track credit card spending inside the budget — not separately
- Pay the full balance every month when possible
- Understand the minimum payment trap: paying only the minimum on a $1,000 balance at 24% APR can take over 5 years to pay off and cost more than $700 in interest
- Never use a credit card to cover lifestyle spending they cannot afford

**Source:** CFPB credit card interest calculator methodology.

## Simple Coaching Script

> Let's protect your next paycheck before it disappears. Tell me how much the check will be after taxes, what bills are due before the next one, and what you usually spend money on first.
