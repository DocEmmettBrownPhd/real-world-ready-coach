# Banking and Credit Framework

The goal is to help young adults enter the banking and credit system safely — without getting trapped by fees, overdrafts, debt, or bad habits.

**Primary sources:** FDIC *Money Smart* program; CFPB consumer tools; myFICO credit education; AnnualCreditReport.com (mandated by the Fair Credit Reporting Act).

---

## Part 1: Opening a Bank Account

### Account Types
- **Checking:** For spending, bills, debit card, direct deposit. Money moves in and out frequently.
- **Savings:** For storing money. Emergency fund, short-term goals. Not meant to be spent regularly.
- **Credit union account:** Member-owned, often lower fees and more forgiving overdraft policies than large banks.
- **Online bank account:** No physical branch, but typically zero monthly fees and higher interest on savings. Examples: Ally, Marcus, Discover Bank.

### What to Compare Before Opening
- Monthly maintenance fee — and whether it can be waived (e.g., with direct deposit)
- Minimum balance requirement
- Overdraft fee amount and whether they auto-enroll you in overdraft protection
- ATM fee and size of fee-free ATM network
- Direct deposit setup process
- Mobile app quality — bill pay, check deposit, alerts
- Account opening requirements (some require a minimum opening deposit)

### FDIC Money Smart Guidance
The FDIC Money Smart curriculum specifically warns young adults about:
- Prepaid debit cards marketed as bank accounts — they often carry high fees without the protections of a real account
- Check-cashing services that charge 1–5% of the check amount — a $1,000 check costs $10–$50 to cash
- Banks that automatically enroll customers in overdraft protection (which charges $25–$35 per overdraft) without clear disclosure

### Red Flags
- Monthly fees that cannot be waived under any condition
- Overdraft fees above $30 per incident
- Minimum balance requirements the user cannot maintain
- Confusing or hard-to-find fee schedule
- Pressure to add products (insurance, investment accounts) they do not need

### Coaching Script
> The goal is to find an account that lets you get paid, pay bills, and avoid fees. Do you need a physical branch, or are you comfortable with online banking? Let's compare two or three options before you open anything.

## Part 2: Understanding Credit

### Simple Explanation
Credit is your track record of borrowing money and paying it back. A credit score is the three-digit number (300–850) lenders use to decide how risky it is to lend to you — and at what interest rate.

### What Credit Affects
- Car loan approvals and interest rate (a score difference of 100 points can mean 3–5% APR difference on a car loan)
- Apartment applications — many landlords run credit checks
- Credit card approvals and limits
- Some utility deposits (low scores may require a larger deposit)
- Some insurance pricing in certain states
- Some employment background checks (certain industries)

### The Five FICO Score Factors
*(Source: myFICO credit education)*

1. **Payment history — 35%** — The single biggest factor. One 30-day late payment can drop a score by 50–100 points.
2. **Credit utilization — 30%** — How much of available credit is in use. Keep below 30%, ideally below 10% for best scores.
3. **Length of credit history — 15%** — How long accounts have been open. Older is better. Do not close old accounts unless necessary.
4. **Credit mix — 10%** — Having different types (credit card, installment loan) shows responsible management of multiple account types.
5. **New credit inquiries — 10%** — Each hard inquiry (from a new application) can temporarily lower the score. Multiple applications in a short period signal financial stress.

### Beginner Credit Rules
- Pay every bill on time — this is the single most important action
- Keep balances well below the credit limit (under 30% of the limit)
- Do not apply for multiple cards at the same time
- Check credit reports for errors once a year at AnnualCreditReport.com — it is federally mandated free
- Set up autopay or calendar payment reminders
- Treat credit as a tool, not as supplemental income

## Part 3: First Credit Card

### Safe First-Card Options
- **Secured credit card** — requires a cash deposit that becomes the credit limit. Low risk. Reports to credit bureaus the same as a regular card.
- **Student credit card** — designed for limited credit history, typically lower limits and more forgiving approval requirements
- **Credit union starter card** — often lower APR and fees than major bank first cards
- **No-annual-fee beginner card** — Chase Freedom Rise, Discover it Student, Capital One Quicksilver Student are commonly cited options
- **Authorized user** — being added to a responsible adult's account builds credit history without needing approval

### First Card Habit Plan
*(Adapted from FDIC Money Smart and CFPB credit card guidance)*

1. Pick one small monthly use — gas, groceries, or phone bill
2. Keep the balance well below the credit limit
3. Set a calendar reminder for the due date
4. Pay the full balance every single month — not just the minimum
5. Never charge what you cannot already cover in cash
6. Review the monthly statement — catch errors and track spending

### Red Flags on First Card Offers
- Annual fees on a beginner card (there are many good cards with zero annual fee)
- APR above 25% without a plan to pay in full every month
- Store cards opened at checkout under pressure — they typically have the highest APRs
- Cash advances — the fee and interest start immediately, often at a higher rate than purchases
- Paying only the minimum — this is how $1,000 becomes $1,700 over five years

### Coaching Script
> I want you to build credit without building debt. The safest first-card habit is to put one small planned expense on the card — something you would buy anyway — then pay it off in full every month. What would you use it for, and how would you make sure it gets paid?

## Part 4: Credit Score Coaching

### No Credit History
No credit is not the same as bad credit. It means lenders have no track record to evaluate.

Next steps: secured card, student card, or becoming an authorized user on a family member's account. Use lightly. Pay on time. The score typically begins appearing within 3–6 months.

### Bad Credit
Bad credit can be improved — but the first job is to stop new damage before working on recovery.

Next steps: catch up on any late payments, lower card balances below 30% of the limit, avoid new unnecessary applications, dispute errors at AnnualCreditReport.com, create automated payment reminders. Recovery takes months, not weeks — set that expectation.

### Credit Score Recovery Timeline
*(Based on myFICO reported averages)*

- Late payment (30 days): recovers in 9–18 months with on-time payments
- High utilization: can recover within 1–2 billing cycles once balances drop
- Collection account: stays on report 7 years but impact decreases over time
- Bankruptcy: stays on report 7–10 years

### Wants a Higher Score Fast
Be direct: there is no legitimate fast fix. Credit repair companies that promise rapid improvement are often charging for things the user can do themselves for free. Real improvement comes from consistent behavior: pay on time, lower balances, wait for history to grow.
