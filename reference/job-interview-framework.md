# Job Interview Framework

The coach helps young adults prepare for interviews — from first fast food job to first career-level position.

**Primary sources:** STAR method (organizational psychology literature, widely documented in DOL career guidance); LinkedIn Economic Graph research on entry-level hiring; DOL Youth Employment guidance.

---

## Core Principle

Preparation kills nervousness. The coach turns nervous energy into practice.

LinkedIn Economic Graph research on hiring decisions shows that interviewers form a strong initial impression within the first 90 seconds — and that impression is driven less by credentials and more by confidence, specificity, and genuine interest in the role. Preparation directly addresses all three.

---

## Step 1: Understand the Job Before Coaching the Answer

Before coaching any interview answer, ask:
1. What job is the interview for?
2. Is this a first job or a step up in a career?
3. What do you know about the company — have you looked them up?
4. Have you read the full job description?
5. Based on the description, what do you think they are most looking for in this candidate?

This step is not optional. A generic interview answer is worse than no coaching at all — it sounds rehearsed without sounding prepared. The coach needs to know the specific job to make the coaching useful.

---

## Step 2: The Core Three Questions

Every interview — from fast food to corporate — has some version of these three. The user should practice them until the answers feel natural, not memorized.

**Question 1: Tell me about yourself.**

Coach the user to:
- Keep it to 60–90 seconds
- Start with where they are right now (student, recent grad, current role)
- Add one relevant experience or strength that connects to this job
- End with why they want this specific role at this specific company

Example structure:
> "I just graduated from [school] with a focus on [area]. During that time I [one relevant experience]. I'm looking for [what this job offers] and I'm especially interested in [something specific about the company or role]."

What to avoid: a life story, starting from childhood, listing every job, or ending without connecting to the role.

**Question 2: Why do you want this job?**

Coach the user to:
- Be specific — "I need the money" is honest but not effective; find something real
- Connect something about the company (their reputation, mission, products, or culture) to something the user genuinely cares about
- Sound genuine, not scripted — one specific, honest reason is more powerful than three polished reasons

**Question 3: Tell me about a time you handled a challenge or problem.**

Use the STAR method:
- **S**ituation — set the context briefly (what was happening)
- **T**ask — what needed to be done (what was your specific responsibility)
- **A**ction — what you specifically did (use "I" not "we")
- **R**esult — what happened as a result (quantify if possible)

The STAR method works because it forces a complete, specific answer. Vague answers about teamwork and communication without a specific story do not land. A specific story lands every time.

---

## Step 3: Common First-Job Questions

Prepare answers for:
- Are you available on weekends and holidays?
- Can you work a flexible or changing schedule?
- Have you worked in a team or customer-facing environment before?
- How do you handle a difficult customer or coworker?
- What is your greatest strength? (Pick one, make it specific)
- What is an area you are working to improve? (Be honest, not coy — "I'm working on [real thing] by doing [real action]")

---

## Step 4: Questions the User Should Ask the Interviewer

Coach every user to have at least two genuine questions ready. Asking nothing signals disengagement. Asking good questions signals that the candidate is serious.

**Good questions:**
- What does a typical day or week look like in this role?
- What do the strongest people in this position tend to do differently?
- How is performance reviewed, and how often?
- What does growth look like from this role?
- What is the team dynamic like?

**Questions to avoid at a first interview:**
- What does the pay come out to? (Wait until they extend an offer or ask directly)
- How quickly can I get promoted?
- How many sick days or vacation days do I get?

These are valid questions — but they are asked after the offer, not before.

---

## Step 5: Reviewing the Job Offer Before Accepting

This step is critical. Coach the user never to accept on the spot without reviewing.

*(DOL guidance: an employer cannot rescind an offer for asking for time to review it. Asking for 24–48 hours is professional, not rude.)*

Review every offer for:
- Pay rate — is it exactly what was discussed? If salary, what is the hourly equivalent?
- Pay schedule — weekly, biweekly, or semi-monthly?
- Guaranteed hours per week — or is the schedule variable?
- Overtime eligibility
- Benefits: health insurance (when does it start?), dental, vision, 401(k) and employer match
- Start date
- Probationary period — are benefits delayed during probation?
- Noncompete or non-solicitation clause — these can limit future employment in the same field
- At-will employment language (standard in most states — understand what it means)

**Red flags:**
- Commission-only with no base guarantee
- Duties much broader in the written offer than described in the interview
- Required unpaid training before employment begins
- Expenses the employee must pay that are not reimbursed
- Noncompete clause that prevents working in the same industry for 1–2 years after leaving
- Pressure to start immediately without time to review the written offer

---

## Step 6: Moving from First Job to Career Job

Coach the user to think about the transition:
- What skills did this first job build — even the ones that feel minor?
- What is the next step: more responsibility at the same company, a different role, or more education?
- Is the resume updated to include this experience with specific results, not just duties?
- Are there 1–2 references available from this job?
- What does the user want from the next job that this one did not provide?

The first job is not a destination. It is the foundation of a story the user will tell in every future interview.

---

## Coaching Script

> Nervous is normal. Let's turn that into preparation. Tell me what the job is, then answer this like you're in the interview right now: "Tell me about yourself." Don't overthink it — just say what comes out. I'll coach your answer and make it stronger.
