# Renting Framework

Renting an apartment is often a young adult's first major financial commitment. The coach goes deep — not surface level.

**Primary sources:** HUD tenant rights guidance; Nolo Press *Renters' Rights*; National Low Income Housing Coalition research; Eviction Lab at Princeton University.

---

## Core Principle

Rent is not the only number. The coach helps users calculate the full cost of moving in and living there — before they fall in love with an apartment.

Eviction Lab research at Princeton shows the most common trigger for first-time renter eviction is not inability to pay rent — it is failure to understand lease terms, specifically late fees, maintenance responsibilities, and deposit deduction rules that were not explained at move-in.

---

## Step 1: Affordability Check

General guideline from HUD housing counseling standards: rent should not exceed 30% of gross monthly income. For young adults with lower income, the coach uses take-home pay as the more practical baseline.

Example coaching:
> If take-home is $2,000/month, rent at $600 leaves breathing room. At $800 it is tight but workable. At $1,100, there is almost nothing left for food, transportation, and emergencies — and one missed shift creates a crisis.

Always calculate the full monthly cost:
- Monthly rent
- Electric, gas, water (ask which utilities are tenant responsibility)
- Internet
- Renter's insurance (typically $10–$20/month — always worth it)
- Transportation from that location to work or school
- Parking fees if applicable

**Renter's insurance note:** NLIHC research shows most first-time renters skip renter's insurance. A single theft or fire can wipe out everything a young adult owns. At $10–$20/month, this is one of the highest-value protections available.

---

## Step 2: Move-In Cost Calculation

The full move-in cost is almost always more than first month's rent. Nolo Press identifies the following as standard components:

- First month's rent
- Security deposit (typically one to two months' rent, varies by state)
- Last month's rent (required in some states and by some landlords)
- Application fee ($25–$75 typically, sometimes non-refundable)
- Move-in or administrative fee (separate from security deposit)
- Pet deposit if applicable

Coach the user to ask for the exact total move-in cost in writing before submitting an application or any payment.

**HUD guidance:** Security deposit rules — maximum amounts, interest requirements, and return timelines — vary by state. Texas, for example, requires return within 30 days. California requires within 21 days. Coach the user to look up their state's rules.

---

## Step 3: Cosigner Coaching (Apartment)

This conversation is kept entirely separate from the car cosigner conversation. Do not carry assumptions between them.

Ask in sequence:
1. Does your income and credit meet the landlord's stated requirements? (Many landlords require income of 2.5–3x monthly rent.)
2. If not, do you have a family member or trusted adult who could cosign?
3. Does that person understand they are fully responsible for the rent if you do not pay — not partially, fully?
4. Are you comfortable asking that person? What is that relationship like?
5. Is a roommate a realistic alternative to a cosigner?

**Legal note (from Nolo):** A cosigner on an apartment lease takes on full joint and several liability. If the tenant stops paying, the landlord can pursue the cosigner for the entire balance without first going after the tenant. Make sure the user and the cosigner both understand this before any agreement is made.

---

## Step 4: Roommate Considerations

If the user cannot afford the apartment alone:
- A roommate splits cost but adds financial and personal complexity
- Both names on the lease means both are legally responsible for 100% of the rent — not just their share
- Discuss explicitly: what happens if the roommate stops paying or moves out early?
- Recommend a written roommate agreement covering: rent split, utilities, shared expenses, guests, notice to vacate, what happens if one person needs to leave
- Even between close friends, a written agreement protects the relationship
- Confirm with the landlord whether both applicants must independently qualify

---

## Step 5: Lease Review (Deep)

Do not skim the lease. Go through it section by section. Eviction Lab data shows blank spaces, automatic renewal clauses, and vague maintenance language are the three most common sources of landlord-tenant disputes for first-time renters.

**Key sections to cover every time:**

- Lease start and end date, and what happens at the end — does it auto-renew?
- Monthly rent, due date, grace period, late fee amount
- Security deposit amount, conditions for deductions, timeline for return
- What counts as normal wear and tear vs. damage (get examples in writing)
- Maintenance and repair responsibilities — who handles what?
- Appliance repair — if the fridge breaks, who pays?
- Utilities — exactly which ones are tenant responsibility
- Pet policy — breed restrictions, fees, deposit
- Guest policy — how many nights before a guest is considered an unauthorized occupant?
- Subletting rules
- Parking — assigned or first-come, fees
- Early termination penalty — exact dollar amount or calculation method
- Notice required to vacate — typically 30–60 days
- Landlord access rules — how much notice must they give before entering? (Most states require 24 hours.)
- Any verbal promises — get every one of them added to the lease in writing before signing

**Lease Red Flags (from HUD and Nolo):**
- Blank spaces anywhere in the document — fill or cross out before signing
- Tenant responsible for structural or major system repairs (roof, plumbing, HVAC)
- No clear security deposit return process or timeline
- Automatic renewal without a clear written opt-out notice requirement
- Requests to pay deposit or first month through wire transfer, Zelle, Venmo, or cash with no receipt
- No property management contact information or physical address
- Pressure to sign the same day without time to review
- Terms significantly different from what was described during the showing

---

## Step 6: Questions to Ask the Landlord Before Signing

1. What is the exact total move-in cost — every fee and deposit?
2. Which utilities am I responsible for, and what do they typically run per month?
3. Who handles maintenance requests and what is the typical response time?
4. Who is responsible for appliance repair if something breaks?
5. What is the exact process for getting my security deposit back?
6. What counts as normal wear and tear versus damage in your view?
7. What is the early termination fee if I need to leave before the lease ends?
8. Can I get a move-in condition report to document the unit's state before I take the keys?
9. Can any verbal promises we discussed be added to the lease in writing?

---

## Coaching Script

> Rent is not the only number. Before you fall in love with this apartment, let's figure out the full monthly cost and the full move-in cost. What is your take-home pay each month, and how much do you have saved right now?
